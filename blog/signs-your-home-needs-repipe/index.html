5 Signs Your Boca Raton Home Needs a Repipe

By the Boca Raton 24/7 Plumbing team · September 17, 2026

Repiping a whole house is a significant project, and most homeowners understandably want to avoid it if a smaller repair would do. But there's a point where patching individual leaks as they happen actually costs more, and creates more disruption, than addressing the underlying pipe condition once. Here are the signs that suggest you're past that point and heading toward a full repipe conversation.

1. You've had more than one leak in the past year or two

A single isolated leak doesn't necessarily mean anything beyond bad luck at one specific joint or fitting. A second or third leak within a short window, especially in different parts of the house, is a different signal entirely. It suggests the pipe material itself is degrading broadly rather than one spot failing randomly, which means more leaks are likely coming even if you can't predict exactly where the next one will show up.

2. Your water has a metallic taste or a rusty tinge

This is a fairly direct sign of internal pipe corrosion, especially in homes with original galvanized steel supply lines. As galvanized pipe corrodes internally, it can affect water taste and occasionally color, particularly noticeable after periods when water has sat in the pipes, like first thing in the morning. This doesn't necessarily mean contamination that's unsafe to drink, but it does mean the pipe interior is deteriorating, and that deterioration eventually leads to reduced flow and, eventually, failure at the weakest points in the system.

3. Water pressure has dropped noticeably over time

Corrosion and mineral buildup narrow the interior diameter of aging pipes, gradually restricting flow even without an actual leak present. If pressure has dropped slowly over years rather than suddenly, and a plumber has ruled out a supply-side issue with the municipal connection itself, internal pipe restriction from corrosion is a common and often overlooked explanation.

4. Visible pipes show significant corrosion or discoloration

  • • Greenish staining on copper pipe joints, a sign of ongoing corrosion at that connection.
  • • Flaking, rust-colored buildup on galvanized steel pipe.
  • • Visible dimpling or pitting on the pipe's exterior surface.
  • • Any exposed pipe, in a garage, utility closet, or crawlspace, that looks noticeably different from a healthy, smooth pipe surface.

If you can see this kind of deterioration on exposed sections, it's a reasonable assumption that similar aging is happening on pipe sections you can't see, hidden inside walls or under the slab.

What happens if you keep patching instead

The math on repeated spot repairs tends to catch up with people eventually. Each individual repair might seem reasonable in isolation, a few hundred dollars here, a service call there. But added up over several incidents on an aging system, the total cost often approaches or exceeds what a planned repipe would have cost. That's on top of the added stress of dealing with each leak as an unplanned emergency rather than a scheduled project on your own timeline.

5. Your home's plumbing predates 1970, and nothing's been replaced since

Age alone isn't a defect, plenty of older pipe is still functioning fine. But copper supply lines have a realistic working lifespan of roughly 50 years, sometimes longer, sometimes shorter depending on water chemistry and how the original installation was done. Galvanized steel, used in many homes built before the 1960s, generally has a shorter practical lifespan than copper, often 40-50 years before corrosion becomes a serious concern. If your home's original plumbing has never been touched and falls into either of these age ranges, it's worth a professional assessment even without an active symptom yet, since catching deterioration before it causes a leak is always less disruptive than reacting to one.

What a professional assessment actually involves

Rather than guessing based on age and symptoms alone, a plumber can directly assess pipe condition through a combination of visual inspection of accessible sections, water pressure testing, and sometimes a camera inspection for sections that aren't visible. This gives you an actual, evidence-based picture of how much time you likely have rather than a vague sense of "it's old, so it's probably fine" or the opposite overreaction of assuming every old pipe needs immediate replacement.

Repiping is also a chance to bring your system up to current code

Florida's plumbing code, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), has evolved considerably since most original 1960s and 1970s-era systems were installed. A full repipe is a natural opportunity to bring the entire system into compliance with current standards rather than working around outdated fittings and materials indefinitely. This includes updated pipe sizing for better flow, current material standards, and proper support and securing methods that weren't always followed decades ago.

Partial repipe versus whole-home: when each makes sense

Not every situation calls for replacing every pipe in the house at once. If deterioration is genuinely isolated to one section, a specific run feeding an addition, for example, a partial repipe addressing just that section can be the more sensible approach. But if the symptoms described above point toward broad, system-wide aging rather than one isolated problem area, a partial fix often just delays the same conversation. Another section tends to fail a year or two later, and by then you've paid for two separate projects instead of one more efficient one done properly from the start.

Thinking it might be time?

Our whole-home repiping page covers what the process actually involves, and if you're not yet sure whether you're at that point, leak detection can assess your current pipe condition without committing to a full project. This comes up frequently in Boynton Beach's original 1970s-era neighborhoods, where original galvanized and early copper installations are now well into their expected replacement window.

Whichever signs brought you here, an honest assessment costs far less than continuing to guess, and it gives you real information to plan around instead of reacting to the next leak whenever it happens.
