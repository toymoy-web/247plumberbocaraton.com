Common Causes

What's usually behind a failing water heater

Sediment buildup

Boca Raton's water leaves mineral sediment in the tank over time, which can insulate the burner or element and cause popping sounds and slower heating.

A failed heating element or thermostat

The most common reason for lukewarm or no hot water in an electric tank, usually a same-visit fix.

A leaking tank

Water pooling at the base, especially near the garage slab edge, often means the tank itself has failed and needs replacement rather than repair.

On gas units, a pilot light that won't stay lit is usually a failed thermocouple, a small safety sensor that shuts off gas flow if it doesn't detect a flame. That's typically an inexpensive same-visit part swap, not a sign the whole unit is failing.

Repair vs. Replace

When repair makes sense, and when it doesn't

A thermostat, heating element, or gas valve replacement is usually the more affordable path and can extend a tank's working life by several years. A tank that's actively leaking from the tank body itself, rather than a fitting or valve, generally can't be repaired since the steel tank has already corroded through, and that's when replacement becomes the honest recommendation rather than a temporary patch.

Most tank water heaters in South Florida's climate last 8 to 12 years. If yours is in that range and showing more than one symptom at once, replacement often costs less over time than repeated repairs.

Before You Call

A quick check that can save a trip

  • • For electric units, check the breaker before assuming the heater itself failed.
  • • For gas units, check whether the pilot light is lit and the gas supply valve is open.
  • • If water is actively leaking from the tank, shut off both the water supply and power/gas to the unit.

Code & Safety

Why the temperature and pressure relief valve matters

Every tank water heater is required by Florida's plumbing code, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), to have a working temperature and pressure relief valve. That's the small valve near the top of the tank that releases pressure if the water gets dangerously hot or the tank overpressurizes. A valve that's stuck, corroded, or missing its discharge pipe is one of the most common code violations we find during a repair visit. It's a safety issue, not just a code checkbox, since a failed valve is what allows pressure to build dangerously in the first place.

If your unit is more than five years old and the relief valve has never been tested, it's worth asking us to check it during any repair visit, even if it isn't the reason you called.
